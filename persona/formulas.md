# Formulas (from analysis of high-performing long-form explainer channels)

## Headline formulas (generate 5 variants per video, mix formulas)
1. Urgent future revelation: "The next [timeframe] of [topic]: why even [experts] are [strong emotion]"
2. Year sequence prediction: "[Year], [Year], [Year]: [bold statement about change]"
3. Authority pattern interrupt: "[Surprising statement]. What [insiders] aren't telling you about [hot topic]"
4. Specific number + consequence: "[Number] [things] that [consequence] by [date]"
5. Contrast: "[Common belief] is wrong. [What is actually happening]."

Rules: under 60 characters where possible, front-load the hook (mobile truncates at ~50), include a time element or a number, create tension without lying. No clickbait that the video does not pay off within the first ninety seconds.

## Five elements every strong title has at least three of
future prediction, emotional intensity (fear, excitement, awe), time-bound element, authority signal, unexpected contrast.

## Script sequence (long video; six blocks, playbook item 1)
1. Hook (0:00-0:15): one shocking, named-authority claim stated as fact, then one sentence of why it breaks the usual assumption. The first 40 words must contain the named actor or the number; if they do not, the script is wrong and must be rewritten (average % viewed across longs is 10-16%, so the first sentence carries the whole video).
2. Context beat (next 5-10 s): "That matters because [one-sentence stake]. [Named source] said [paraphrase]." This proves the hook before any framework talk.
3. Core message (the future, the takeaways, what to do): one clear framework the viewer can remember (three stages, two forces, a timeline). Preview it by 0:40, not 1:30 (futurework review 2026-11-22: viewers quit around the authority beat).
4. Evidence: two or more attributed quotes or excerpts from named sources, plus industry examples and market impact. The single strongest piece of evidence goes before the 40% mark, never saved for the end.
5. Application ("why you should care"): connect to the viewer's own next twelve months, one emotionally resonant line. This block comes right before the CTA, never skipped.
6. CTA: one question for the comments, one-sentence subscribe reminder, nothing else. No business pitch.
Open one loop in the hook and pay it off in the last third; end on the application line, not a recap. Do not manufacture conflict the script cannot support.

## Engagement triggers
- A question to the audience inside the video, not only at the end.
- "Is this actually worth it" style debates where honest.
- Specific, named sources create trust; vague "experts say" does not.

## Psychological triggers (use honestly)
future glimpse, expert concern, transformation narrative, clear pattern, acceleration/urgency.

## Shorts formula (updated 2026-11-24 from the network's top 40 Shorts; see growth log)
Line 1: the hook, under twelve words, naming a specific actor, object or number (a claim, a number, or a contradiction). No setup, no "in this video", no question-only opener. Best-performing openers state a checkable fact or an exact answer ("Rice in Russian is ris", "Freight rates rose 342 percent"), not a mood ("Nobody Lost the Pipe" is the anti-pattern: zero traction).
Lines 2-3: the payoff. The viewer must know what the hook meant by the third sentence; the retention cliff on our Shorts sits near 30 seconds, so the surprise comes before it.
Lines 4-6: one idea, one example, one consequence, attributed in-line.
Last line: a question the viewer can answer in one comment, or a line that loops back to the first sentence so the Short rewatches cleanly.
75-110 words, about 35-45 seconds spoken. One idea per Short, never a summary of the long video.

## Title rules that separate winners from losers (all five channels, 2026-11-24 scoreboard)
- The searchable answer or subject sits in the first three words. Winners: "Rice in Russian: ris", "Freight up 342 percent. Demand isn't.", "The rescue didn't doom Swift". Losers: "Being Right Is No Longer Enough", "The Two Column Job Check" (concepts with no searchable noun, and no number anywhere).
- Put the exact payoff word/number in the title when it fits: "Salt in Japanese: shio" was the fastest-starting Short on the network (369 views/day in its first day). A title that already answers the question still gets the click because searchers want confirmation plus context.
- Contradiction titles beat mood titles: "Teams wasn't hacked. That's the problem.", "Freight up 342 percent. Demand isn't." Both state a fact, then flip an assumption.
- Never open a title with a gerund or an adjective ("Being Right...", "Ignore the headline...", "Learn a Trade Is Half Right" mixed it with a fact and still underperformed).
- Question titles die unless they contain the searchable subject verbatim ("Where can a falling telescope land?" got 3 views in two weeks; "Could a Moon launch move your car's GPS?" held 12, and both lacked the exact search phrase).

## Voice delivery rules (all channels, from winners' 40-50% average-viewed Shorts)
- The first sentence is slower information, not faster hype: it delivers the who/what number the viewer searched for. Numbers in the first line, not at the end.
- One idea per sentence; the longest sentence comes mid-Short, never in the first two.
- End sentences on the strong word (the number, the name), not on a preposition or hedge.

## Thumbnail text
Max three words. All caps. One emotional word allowed. Must make sense next to the title without repeating it.

## Writing Rules Shared With Articles
Each block of the six-block sequence must introduce a genuinely different angle. Every paragraph adds information instead of restating an earlier point. Write simple, natural spoken English without filler, AI phrasing, generic transitions or repetitive conclusions. Hedge uncertain causes with "can", "may", or "may indicate". Attribute every statistic in-line to a named source. Keep claims bounded by the evidence; no medical, legal, financial or income promises. Do not use em dashes in narration, captions, titles, descriptions or Shorts. Use punctuation that reads naturally aloud. See `persona/blog-rules.md` for the article-specific structure and SEO checks.
