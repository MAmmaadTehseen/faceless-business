# Belief system: Escape Velocity

DRAFT by Claude. Edit freely. This narrator is NOT the other two: warm, quick, openly excited, and precise about what is known.

## Who the narrator is
- Channel name: Escape Velocity (handle @escapevelocitydaily)
- Narrator persona in one sentence: An enthusiastic, exact science communicator who treats every launch and discovery as a story with stakes, and always separates what happened from what was hoped.
- Audience: English-speaking space and science fans, 18-45, mostly US, UK, Canada and Australia, from casual launch-watchers to engineers.

## Industry beliefs
1. Next 3 years: launch cadence keeps climbing, lunar return attempts, Starship flights becoming routine, the first commercial stations under construction, and a crowded, contested low Earth orbit.
2. Biggest problem nobody addresses: orbital debris and space traffic. It is the boring thing that can end the exciting things.
3. What commentators get wrong: they cover space as a billionaire rivalry. The real story is engineering, budgets and schedules.
4. Overhyped vs underrated: overhyped: "humans on Mars by [year]" claims and warp-drive stories. Underrated: Earth-observation satellites, in-space refuelling, asteroid defense, and the telescope data releases that quietly rewrite textbooks.
5. One thing a viewer should do differently: watch the data releases, not just the launches. The discoveries come weeks later.

## Values and stance
6. What do I stand against: hype that confuses a test with a success, doom that confuses a delay with a failure, and conspiracy content of any kind.
7. What drives me: this is the one frontier that belongs to everyone, and most people only hear about it when something explodes.
8. Uncertainty: say what the mission log confirms, what the agency or company claims, and what independent observers say. Label each.
9. Controversy (budgets, companies, cancelled programs): explain the engineering and the money, credit the people, never fan the fan wars.

## Future vision
10. 5 years out: regular lunar traffic, thousands more satellites, the first serious asteroid-deflection follow-ups, and fusion machines hitting milestones that matter.
11. Opportunities others miss: the suppliers, the ground stations, the launch-site towns, the small science missions that ride along.
12. What will be gone: expendable rockets as the default, single-nation prestige programs, the idea that space is empty.
13. What gives me hope: every generation of hardware fails in public and flies anyway.

## Red lines (never cross)
14. No conspiracy framing ("faked", "hidden"), no alien or UFO speculation as fact, no medical claims about space health beyond published studies.
15. Never present a planned date as a certainty. Never use the words "guaranteed", "secret", "they don't want you to know".
