# A Government Just Budgeted AI Job Cuts. 25,000 of Them.

The Dutch government just put a number on the desk jobs it thinks it no longer needs. Twenty-five thousand. Not a tech startup, not a bank, but a national government, with a savings target, a deadline of 2035, and a template any employer could copy.

The headlines say the Netherlands will replace twenty-five thousand government jobs with AI. That is not quite what was announced. And the gap between the headline and the actual plan is the part you need to understand, because your own employer may already be reading from the same playbook.

Here is what was reported. On Monday, October twenty-sixth, the Dutch cabinet said it wants about twenty-five thousand fewer full-time posts in central government by 2035, according to Bloomberg, citing a government statement. That is roughly sixteen percent of a workforce of about a hundred and sixty thousand full-time posts. The target is a saving of nine hundred million euros a year by 2027, rising to three and a half billion euros a year by 2035.

The plan went to the Dutch parliament from Eric van der Burg, the state secretary responsible for making government work more effectively. He pointed to a string of non-binding motions in parliament that asked for a twenty percent productivity gain through AI, as Bloomberg reported. Police and defence are exempt. So the cuts land on everyone else.

Stay with me, because this is where it turns. The Netherlands is not in a budget crisis. Its deficit is about one point eight percent, which Bloomberg notes is one of the smallest in the European Union. So this is not a government forced to cut. It is a government choosing to move money, and naming AI as part of the reason.

So I sort this plan into three columns. First, what is announced: the numbers, the dates, the tools on the list. Then, what is projected: the savings and productivity that only happen if things go right. And the part that matters, what is a guess: how this actually lands on people. Most coverage blends all three, so let us pull them apart.

Column one, announced. Twenty-five thousand posts, a 2035 deadline, three and a half billion euros a year. But look at what sits next to AI on the list. According to TechRadar's summary, the government also plans to scrap or simplify at least five hundred rules a year, sell surplus buildings, standardise its IT systems, and have pairs of employees share a single desk.

Even the advice layer is in scope. TechRadar reports there are currently twenty-eight permanent advisory councils and about a hundred and fifty advisory committees, and the government will set new targets for them later this year. Frontline security is protected. The people who write the reports are not.

Here is the part most people miss. The AI headline carries more weight than the plan itself puts on it. One independent write-up of the announcement, by tech blogger Pasquale Pillitteri, points out that the savings coming from AI have not been put into numbers. Fewer rules, fewer buildings and fewer desks are doing real work in this budget, and AI is the label that travelled.

There is another number worth sitting with. The same write-up says central government grew from about a hundred and thirty-nine thousand people in 2021 to more than a hundred and sixty-eight thousand last year. That is close to thirty thousand extra people in about four years. So a cut of twenty-five thousand by 2035 looks less like a robot takeover and more like reversing a hiring wave, with AI named as the reason it will not come back.

Now a quick piece of arithmetic, and treat it as arithmetic, not reporting. Three and a half billion divided by twenty-five thousand is about a hundred and forty thousand euros per post. That figure also has to cover buildings, desks and IT, so do not read it as a salary. Read it as a reminder that the savings target reaches well beyond the payroll line.

Column two, projected. The three and a half billion is a target, not a result. The twenty-five thousand figure is described as an estimate, and the split between ministries is expected with the spring budget memo. Bloomberg reports that the cabinet will present its plans for actually deploying AI early next year. In other words, the savings line exists before the AI plan does.

That is the shift from payroll to compute in its purest form. Budgets move before capabilities do. A company does this in a quarterly memo. Here a country did it with a ten-year target, while the tool that is supposed to make it work is still being written up.

Now the strongest argument on the other side, because it is a fair one. Ten years is a long runway. Setting a target first can force departments to redesign their work instead of bolting AI onto old processes. The government also says it wants to work with Dutch and European suppliers, so it is not dependent on technology from outside Europe. That is a deliberate plan, not a panic.

And the cuts will not be spread evenly. The Edge, carrying the Bloomberg report, says some parts of government could face what were called significant staffing implications, with reductions of forty-five or even sixty percent. If you work in one of those units, sixteen percent is not your number.

Column three, the guess, and I will label it clearly because nobody knows yet. My bet is that most of the twenty-five thousand disappears through posts that are never refilled, not through mass layoffs. Ten years of normal retirements and departures adds up in a workforce this size. The real change will be quieter. It is the vacancy that never gets posted.

Now think about the people who depend on those services. The Netherlands has been here before, in a painful way. In the childcare benefits scandal, the tax authority's risk systems helped wrongly flag thousands of families as fraud suspects, and the cabinet resigned over it in January 2021. That history does not prove AI in government will fail, but it shows who pays first when it does.

So the question for citizens is not whether AI gets used. It is who checks it, and how many humans are left to pick up the phone when it gets something wrong. Cutting staff and adding automation at the same time can remove the very people who would catch the errors. That is the risk worth watching when the detailed plan arrives.

Then there are private employers. Every finance director in Europe now has a public example of a large employer writing AI productivity into a headcount target. If the Dutch plan hits its 2027 number, expect that slide in a lot of board decks. If it misses, expect quieter versions of the same idea anyway, because the target was the point.

A quick question while we are here. Has your employer put an actual productivity number on AI yet, or only a list of tools? Tell me in the comments, because that difference says more about your job than any headline.

So what does this mean for your next twelve months, wherever you work? Read your own organisation the way we just read this plan. Ask which numbers are announced, which are projected, and which are guesses dressed up as plans. Then watch which teams stop backfilling roles, and which teams get the new tools first. And ask your manager what success looks like for the AI tools you already have.

Also notice the work the Dutch plan quietly depends on. Somebody has to decide which five hundred rules go. Somebody has to check the AI output, explain it to the people paying for it, and handle the cases it gets wrong. Those translator roles are often where the budget flows next.

The people who find out about a change last always pay the most for it. The Dutch just gave everyone a ten-year warning, in writing. So here is my question. If your employer published a number like twenty-five thousand tomorrow, which column would your job sit in?
